# Generate, curate, then pay humans where it matters

The fastest dependable route for a non-artist to get Gen 3–style fakemon battle sprites in late 2026 has two parts. First, the coding assistant drives a **pixel-native AI generator through its REST API**. Retro Diffusion is the first choice and PixelLab the second. The request asks for an exact 64x64 canvas and a fixed 15-colour palette, and the assistant runs dozens of candidates through the lossless importer and automated Gen 3 rule checks. Second, you **pay a human pixel artist** to fix the shortlisted legendaries, and the back sprites in particular, or to draw them from scratch if the AI drafts don't hold up. Generation costs cents per image, roughly $0.015–$0.18 ([Gamelabs](https://gamelabstudio.co/blog/gamelabs-studio-vs-retro-diffusion); [PixelLab API](https://www.pixellab.ai/pixellab-api)). Hobbyist sprite commissions run about $10–$50 per creature ([Fiverr](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/pokemon-sprite); [2D Will Never Die](https://2dwillneverdie.com/blog/how-much-do-sprites-cost/)). So the 3 legendaries realistically cost **$10–$170 and a few days**, and the later batch of ~30 costs **$50–$750**, depending on how much human finishing you buy. Your assistant's earlier attempts failed for a reason: the community has long judged downscaled drawings "blurry" ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/the-493-gba-project.237375/)), and good sprites come from clean 1-pixel outlines and deliberate shading, which code drawing shapes cannot produce. The remedy is to stop asking the LLM to draw. Let it orchestrate generators, enforce the rules, and do the index-level work it is genuinely good at: palette remaps, recolours, compositing and validation. Before you generate anything, make one decision. pokeemerald-expansion ships **Gen 4/5-style sprites by default** and needs `P_GBA_STYLE_SPECIES_GFX TRUE` to show true GBA style ([pokeemerald-expansion config](https://github.com/rh-hideout/pokeemerald-expansion/blob/master/include/config/pokemon.h)), and your fakemon must match whichever style the other 386+ species use.

## Gen 3 sprites follow a strict, checkable rulebook

The style rules are concrete enough to encode as automated checks. That matters because your assistant cannot judge a sprite by eye, but it can reject candidates that break measurable rules. Gen 3 front and back battle sprites both sit on a **64x64 canvas** ([PokéBase](https://pokemondb.net/pokebase/418580/what-are-the-dimensions-all-the-sizes-the-gen-battle-sprites)). Within it, official R/S figures fall into rough **40, 48, 56 and 64 px size classes**, so a legendary should fill most of the frame ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/sprite-sizes.340457/)). A sprite has 16 palette slots, and index 0 is transparent, which leaves **15 visible colours** ([PokéCommunity tutorial](https://www.pokecommunity.com/showthread.php?p=9957322)). Front and back must share that one palette ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/211085)). The Pokémon faces left, with light from the top-left ([Pokébip](https://www.pokebip.com/page__trucage__tuto_sprite.html)). Outlines are never all black. They turn black where they meet shadow and use the darkest shade of the local colour where they meet lit areas ([PIF Spriting Guidelines](https://hackmd.io/@PIF-Staff/SpritingGuidelines)).

Each material should use **no more than four shades**: highlight, main, shadow and an outline tone. More than that makes sprites look "blurry or as if the files have been saved wrong" ([PIF](https://hackmd.io/@PIF-Staff/SpritingGuidelines)). With 15 colours, that caps a design at roughly three or four distinct materials. Legendary concepts with five colour zones need ramps that share shades. Anti-aliasing should appear only as single pixels in interior corners, never as a soft edge against the transparent background. The two classic beginner mistakes are pillow shading, where shading runs parallel to the edges toward a bright centre, and banding ([Logbook Project](https://the-logbook-project.blogspot.com/2013/04/pixel-art-lessons-jiinchus-darkness.html); [Pixnote](https://pixnote.net/en/learn/glossary/)). Sources disagree on dithering. One summary says Gen 3 uses a lot of it, while fan rule sets such as Smogon CAP allow it only "sparingly" ([Smogon CAP](https://www.smogon.com/cap/process/events/sprite_submissions)). Treat light dithering as acceptable but optional.

| Rule | Value | Automatable check |
|---|---|---|
| Canvas | 64x64, index 0 transparent | Image size, alpha only on index 0 |
| Colours | ≤15 visible, shared front/back (pret's guide suggests ≤14 to be safe) | Unique-colour count; palettes identical |
| Legendary footprint | ~56–64 px bounding box | Bounding-box size |
| Facing / light | Left-facing, top-left light | Human review |
| Outline | 1 px, unbroken, coloured (black only on shadow side) | Outline continuity, orphan-pixel scan |
| Shades per material | ≤4 including outline tone | Palette clustering |
| Edge AA | None against transparency | No semi-dark halo pixels on the silhouette edge |

Hand-pixeling follows three routes, ranked by community respect. The first is a true **scratch** sprite: sketch, clean 1 px outline, flat fills, top-left shading, outline shading, then cleanup. The second is a **pixel-over** of your own drawing, tracing the outline pixel by pixel over the concept on the canvas. The third is a **splice** of official sprites ([Smogon pixel-over guide](https://www.smogon.com/smog/issue10/spriting); [Dragonfly Cave](https://www.dragonflycave.com/spriting-guide/)). The tools are cheap. **Aseprite costs $19.99 one-time** and is the de facto standard ([Steambase](https://steambase.io/games/aseprite/price)). LibreSprite, Pixelorama and GraphicsGale are free ([MyNixOS](https://mynixos.com/nixpkgs/package/libresprite); [PortableApps](https://portableapps.com/node/80848); [GameDev.net](https://www.gamedev.net/news/graphicsgale-goes-freeware-r146/)). The skill is the expensive part. A first simple sprite takes a beginner an hour or two ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/431730/discussions/0/597392317408651120)), but critique threads show it takes many sprites and many rounds of feedback before outlines and shading pass community standards. This report found no reliable published figure for hours per Gen 3 fakemon sprite. The best reference guides are the PIF staff guidelines, Dragonfly Cave, the PokéCommunity spriting tutorials and Smogon's Smog articles. For general fundamentals, Saint11 suits low resolutions and Slynyrd suits higher ones ([saint11.art](https://saint11.art/blog/pixel-art-tutorials/)).

## Splicing is code-friendly, but free packs rarely fit Gen 3

A splice takes a base body sprite, pastes parts from other official sprites onto it, redraws the seams, unifies the light direction and recolours everything to one palette ([PokeCharms](https://pokecharms.com/threads/toastas-tutorial-on-the-basics-of-spriting.965/)). Dragonfly Cave warns against reusing the same part twice and against flipping a part without re-lighting it, and says that scaling pixel art with paint tools "always looks terrible" ([Dragonfly Cave](https://www.dragonflycave.com/spriting-guide/)). Critics spot weak splices by the "stamped on" look of parts that keep their original shading ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/my-first-try-with-sprite-splicing-did-i-say-that-right.163400/)). Expansion's own tutorial effectively starts every new species as an edit: copy an existing species folder such as Mew's, then edit `anim_front.png` and `back.png` in indexed mode with 15 colours ([how_to_new_pokemon.md](https://github.com/rh-hideout/pokeemerald-expansion/blob/master/docs/tutorials/how_to_new_pokemon.md)). Splicing suits your assistant better than freehand drawing, because recolouring by palette-index remap, mirroring, compositing and merging palettes are exact operations a program does well. Seam repair and re-shading still need an eye. That makes splices a fit for minor fakemon and regional variants, not for showpiece legendaries.

Community norms split sharply. Pokémon Infinite Fusion is built on splicing, with more than 200,000 custom sprites from over 6,000 artists ([FusionDex](https://www.fusiondex.org/)). Its rules let anyone use base sprites freely but require explicit permission to reuse anyone's fusion sprite ([PIF](https://hackmd.io/@PIF-Staff/SpritingGuidelines)). Smogon CAP bans fusions and pixel-overs of others' line art outright ([CAP 36](https://www.smogon.com/forums/threads/cap-36-part-12-sprite-submissions-transformation.3766594/)). The near-universal etiquette is to credit every pack and artist, ask before editing anyone's custom work, never present edits as your own, and stay non-profit ([PokéFarm Q](https://pokefarm.com/forum/thread/171641/Sprite-Permission-Request-Thread/1); [PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/should-fakemon-be-shared.398695/)). Relic Castle's DMCA takedown in March 2024 is a reminder that Nintendo enforcement can reach fan hubs regardless of these norms ([VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/pokemon-fan-game-repository-taken-down-by-a-reported-dmca-notice)).

Free fakemon packs exist, but nearly all are DS-sized (Gen 4/5) and built for Pokémon Essentials. **No free pack in explicit Gen 3 GBA style turned up.** The most useful are Screen Smith's **Openmon** sets, native 64x64 battle sprites with Sets 1 and 3 under **CC0** ([Openmon Set 3](https://screensmith.itch.io/openmon-monster-sprites-set-3); [Set 1](https://screensmith.itch.io/openmon-monster-sprites-set-1)). Next come Mikitari's 80+ Gen 4–style fakemon with back sprites, "free to use in any project" ([Eevee Expo 1517](https://eeveeexpo.com/resources/1517/)). Others are the 115-design **Fakemon Festival Pack**, which requires crediting both the pack and the original designers ([Eevee Expo 654](https://eeveeexpo.com/resources/654/)), and the **Earthretha** pack, free to use and edit with credit ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/earthretha-fakemon-pack.527306/)). DS sprites must be cropped or redrawn down to 64x64, never scaled, and re-indexed to 15 colours. Most packs lack back sprites or second animation frames. They fit expansion's default Gen 4/5 look better than a GBA-style hack. Use them as filler for the later 30, not as legendaries.

## AI pixel tools now produce true grids, but not battle backs

Two purpose-built tools lead in 2026, and both expose APIs your assistant can call. **Retro Diffusion** generates native pixel art at exact sizes. Its v2 API (`POST /v2/inferences`, `X-RD-Token` header) accepts width and height down to 12 px for some RD Pro styles. Other parameters include an `input_palette` image that constrains colours, `return_pre_palette` for comparing raw and quantized output, `remove_bg`, an optional `input_image`, and up to **nine reference images** on RD Pro. Feeding an accepted output back in as a reference is the documented way to keep a character consistent ([Retro-Diffusion/api-examples](https://github.com/Retro-Diffusion/api-examples)). It charges per image from a prepaid balance: RD Fast about $0.015–0.04, RD Plus about $0.025–0.06, and **RD Pro about $0.18**. New accounts get $0.50 free ([Gamelabs](https://gamelabstudio.co/blog/gamelabs-studio-vs-retro-diffusion)). **PixelLab** offers a web app, an Aseprite extension, a v2 REST API and an official MCP server ([PixelLab MCP](https://github.com/pixellab-code/pixellab-mcp)). Its standout feature is 4/8-direction rotation from a reference image. The API costs about **$0.017 per 64x64 eight-direction character** ([PixelLab API](https://www.pixellab.ai/pixellab-api)). Subscriptions are reported at roughly $12, $24 and $50 per month, with a 40-generation free trial, though third-party listings disagree on the details ([Knowara](https://knowara.com/ai-tools/image/pixellab-review/)). Both vendors grant you ownership of outputs, including for commercial use ([PixelLab FAQ](https://www.pixellab.ai/docs/faq); [retrodiffusion.ai](https://retrodiffusion.ai/)).

General models are a different matter. Midjourney, GPT-image, Gemini and Leonardo produce painterly "pixel-look" images whose cells don't line up on a lattice ([Retro-Diffusion/pixel-art-fixer](https://github.com/Retro-Diffusion/pixel-art-fixer)). That is the same failure as your assistant's downscaling experiment, and reviewers treat these models as "inspiration only" ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/ai-pixel-art-generators)). Open SDXL LoRAs exist, including "Pokemon Sprites XL", which outputs a front-and-back pair, but they date from 2023–24 and output 96x96 cells, not Gen 3's 64x64 ([Civitai](https://civitai.com/models/255424/pokemon-sprites-xl)).

The evidence on quality is thin. **No independent benchmark of 64x64 Pokémon-style creatures exists.** One reviewer calls PixelLab the only tool that reliably produces usable pixel art ([jonathanyu.xyz](https://www.jonathanyu.xyz/2025/12/31/pixellab-review-the-best-ai-tool-for-2d-pixel-art-games/)). Another sums it up as "80% of a sprite set in minutes. The last 20% is fixing the frames where the character's silhouette wandered" ([Techsy](https://techsy.io/en/blog/meshy-vs-higgsfield-vs-pixellab)). The biggest gap is the back sprite. **Neither tool has a battle-back mode.** PixelLab's "north" rotation is a top-down RPG view, not the larger, cropped, over-the-shoulder Gen 3 back sprite. On Retro Diffusion, the best option is to pass the accepted front sprite as a reference and prompt for a rear view. Expect the back sprite to need the most human correction. If cleanup tools are needed at all, Sprite Fusion's open-source Pixel Snapper, which quantizes to a custom palette, and Retro Diffusion's pixel-art-fixer handle off-grid output ([Pixel Snapper](https://github.com/Hugo-Dz/spritefusion-pixel-snapper)). Native 64x64 output should go straight into your existing lossless importer. One caveat if you ever publish: PokéCommunity bars generative-AI content in fan games posted there, even as placeholders ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/fakemon-designer-spriter-looking-to-help.477883/)). Infinite Fusion forbids AI "in any stage of spriting" ([PIF](https://hackmd.io/@PIF-Staff/SpritingGuidelines)). For a private hack this costs you nothing, but it does close off those venues later.

## Commissions cost $10–$50 per creature at the hobbyist tier

The commission market for this niche is real and cheap. Fiverr sellers who explicitly offer GBA, Gen 3 or ROM-hack battle sprites include samvicasoge, taiwosamvic, pollorenz (64x64 fakemon), akinenoch771, cunnox10, mayohgold and pelumi_jem. Gigs start around **$10–$30**, and front+back+icon tiers reach about **$50** ([Fiverr category](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/pokemon-sprite); [pollorenz](https://fiverr.com/pollorenz/make-sprite-of-custom-pokemon); [samvicasoge](https://nl.fiverr.com/samvicasoge)). Back sprites are often a separate add-on, so the brief must list every asset. Independent artists with Carrd price sheets, paid through VGen or Ko-fi, quote anywhere from **$8–$13 per fakemon character** at the low end ([heinanaing](https://heinanaing-fakemon-commissions.carrd.co/)) to **$30–$50 per sprite for skilled artists** ([2D Will Never Die](https://2dwillneverdie.com/blog/how-much-do-sprites-cost/)). Some add **+50% for commercial use**, a surcharge a free private hack should avoid ([mich-spich](https://mich-spich.carrd.co)). Turnaround is about **a week per design** ([heinanaing](https://heinanaing-fakemon-commissions.carrd.co/)). Artists are also listed in PokéCommunity showcase threads such as Dvor-ak's ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/dvor-ak-sprites.425794/)), the Reborn Community Commission Index ([Reborn](https://www.rebornevo.com/clubs/82-community-commission-index/)) and the PMDCollab list ([PMDCollab](https://hacknews.pmdcollab.org/old/comm_list.html)). PokéCommunity's pixel-art forum is for showcases, not recruitment ([PokéCommunity](https://www.pokecommunity.com/threads/seeking-spriters-for-rom-hack-collaboration.255151/)).

| Scope | Hobbyist / Fiverr tier | Established independent |
|---|---|---|
| 3 legendaries, front + back + shiny palette | ~$30–$150 | ~$150–$400+ |
| ~30 further fakemon (bulk rate $10–$25 each) | ~$300–$750 | Quote per artist |

*These budget figures are extrapolated from single-artist examples; no 2025–2026 price survey exists.*

There are two pitfalls. First, **some Fiverr sellers in this niche mention using AI tools** ([Fiverr](https://www.fiverr.com/gigs/pokemon-sprite)). Ask for a sketch stage and WIPs, and ask for one substantive revision ([Writers Fun Zone](https://writersfunzone.com/blog/2026/03/20/how-to-spot-scammers-and-ai-artists-in-your-book-art-search-green-flags-vs-red-flags-by-anna-leitensdorfer/)). Second, refund terms are strict, for example "no refunds after sketch approval" ([natnatcommissions](https://natnatcommissions.carrd.co/)). A good brief includes the concept art, the size class ("legendary, fills ~60 px"), a 64x64 indexed PNG with index 0 transparent and ≤15 colours (≤14 to be safe), front and back sharing one palette, and a shiny palette as a pure index swap delivered as a `.pal` or hex list. Add 2–3 official Emerald sprites of a similar body type as style references, a second front frame for `anim_front.png` if wanted, and written terms covering free, non-commercial ROM-hack use, the credit line and the revision count ([pret wiki](https://github.com/pret/pokeemerald/wiki/How-to-add-a-new-Pok%C3%A9mon-species)).

## The ranked strategy: AI drafts, human finish, then scale

The ranking below weighs four things: what the assistant can do unaided, what quality legendaries deserve, cost, and how well each option repeats across 30 more creatures.

| Rank | Strategy | Role | Cost | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Retro Diffusion API (PixelLab as backup), driven by the assistant with automated rule checks | Drafts for all 33; final art for good-enough results | ~$5–$15 for the 3 legendaries; ~$30–$80 for 30 more | Hours |
| 2 | Commission a human to fix the best AI drafts, or to sprite the legendaries from scratch | Legendaries and all back sprites | ~$30–$150 (3 legendaries, hobbyist) | ~1–2 weeks |
| 3 | Bulk deal with the same artist for the later 30 | Consistent style across the dex | ~$300–$750 | Weeks |
| 4 | Assistant-built splices and recolours of official sprites | Regional variants, minor fakemon, shiny palettes | Free | Hours each, plus manual seam fixes |
| 5 | Free packs (Openmon CC0, Mikitari, Earthretha, Fakemon Festival) | Filler commons; reference palettes | Free, with credit | Crop and re-index per sprite |
| 6 | Learn to pixel yourself (Aseprite $19.99 or LibreSprite) | Long-term independence; touch-ups | $0–$20 | Months to competence |

**Step 0, today, 10 minutes: choose the style flag.** If you keep expansion's default Gen 4/5-style sprites, your fakemon should look DS-style, and the free DS packs become direct fits. If you want true Gen 3, set `P_GBA_STYLE_SPECIES_GFX TRUE` so the official Pokémon match ([config](https://github.com/rh-hideout/pokeemerald-expansion/blob/master/include/config/pokemon.h)). Every prompt, reference and commission brief depends on this choice.

**Step 1, day 1, about $5–$15: run the AI pipeline for the 3 legendaries.** Fund a Retro Diffusion API key with $10, then have the assistant query the styles endpoint for each style's size limits. The assistant should build a palette PNG for each legendary: 15 colours, GBA 5-bit values, roughly three materials with four shades each. It then calls RD Pro or RD Plus at 64x64 with `input_palette`, `remove_bg`, the concept image as `input_image` or a reference, and two or three official Emerald front sprites of a similar body plan as extra references. Prompts should describe "left-facing, three-quarter view, light from top-left". Don't put "pixel art" in the prompt ([api-examples](https://github.com/Retro-Diffusion/api-examples)). Generate about 10–20 candidates per legendary. Your lossless importer then reads each one, and a script rejects any candidate that breaks the table's checkable rules: colour count, footprint, outline continuity, orphan pixels, edge halos. You pick the best two or three by eye. For backs, pass the chosen front as a reference and prompt for a rear view. Also run PixelLab's 40 free generations on the same concepts to compare. At $0.18 per RD Pro image, about 60–80 images costs about $11–$15.

**Step 2, days 2–10, about $30–$150: buy the human finish.** Rate the AI results honestly against the in-game official sprites. If the fronts read well, commission a Gen 3 specialist such as one of the Fiverr GBA sellers or a PokéCommunity showcase artist. Ask them to clean the outlines and shading and to draw proper battle backs on the shared palette. Attach the AI drafts as concept references and say openly that the drafts are AI-made. If the fronts don't read well, commission all three from scratch with the brief above. Either way, ask for one test sprite before paying for all three. Your converter then produces the 4bpp graphics, `normal.pal` and `shiny.pal`. Expansion expects two-frame `anim_front.png` files unless you use a one-frame `front.png` the way Pecharunt does ([how_to_new_pokemon.md](https://github.com/rh-hideout/pokeemerald-expansion/blob/master/docs/tutorials/how_to_new_pokemon.md)). A simple second frame shifted by 1 px is something the assistant can produce itself.

**Step 3, later, about $50–$750 for the ~30.** Re-run the Step 1 pipeline in batch. Reuse the artist's finished palettes and legendary sprites as Retro Diffusion references, or as a custom style, so the AI drafts inherit the artist's look. Sort the results into three tiers. Ship-as-is commons get light cleanup only. Mid-tier designs go to the same artist at a negotiated bulk rate of $10–$25 for each cleanup or sprite. Variants become assistant-made recolours and splices of existing sprites. Crediting free-pack sprites (Openmon CC0, Mikitari) can fill gaps if you chose DS style in Step 0. Keep a credits file listing every pack, artist and AI tool used.

The ranking rests on this logic. Rank 1 is first because it is the only option that is near-free, immediate and fully executable by your assistant through APIs. It also turns "can't see to draw" into "can generate many candidates and filter by measurable rules." Rank 2 sits right behind it because the evidence shows AI gets you about 80%, and legendaries, especially their back sprites, live in the missing 20%. The main uncertainty is quality. No head-to-head test of 64x64 Pokémon-style output exists, so Step 1 is also a cheap experiment that tells you how much Step 2 you need to buy.

## Conclusion

The core change in approach is to stop treating the LLM as an artist and treat it as a production manager. An assistant that cannot draw can still call pixel-native generators, enforce a 15-colour shared palette, reject candidates that break measurable Gen 3 rules, write the shiny palettes, build the second animation frames and convert the result to 4bpp. Each of those is a deterministic task. That leaves human skill only where it is irreplaceable, in outline polish and in over-the-shoulder back sprites. At hobbyist commission rates, that human part costs tens of dollars per creature, not hundreds.

The decision with the most leverage costs nothing: matching your fakemon to the style expansion is actually configured to show. A beautiful Gen 3 legendary next to Gen 5–style starters will look more wrong than a mediocre sprite in a consistent style. Settle the `P_GBA_STYLE_SPECIES_GFX` question first. Then spend the first $10 on generation, so you see what the AI can do before you pay a human for the rest.
